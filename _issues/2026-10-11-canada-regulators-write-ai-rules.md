## In brief

- Canada's federal privacy reform bill has not moved since June, and there is no federal AI law.
- Regulators filled the gap, finding that OpenAI's early ChatGPT training and deepfakes made with Grok, the AI tool on X, did not comply with existing privacy law.
- Québec's modern privacy law has no confirmed penalty yet ⚠️ verify, and British Columbia's private-sector law is unchanged ⚠️ verify.

## The story so far

Canada's federal private-sector privacy law is the Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA). Its regulator is the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada (OPC), and Québec, British Columbia (BC) and Alberta have their own laws and commissioners. The last attempt to replace PIPEDA and add an AI law, Bill C-27, lapsed when Parliament's session ended on 2025-01-06. Its successor, Bill C-36, had first reading on 2026-06-15.

Québec's privacy law, Law 25, is fully in force, with large penalties on paper ⚠️ verify. BC's Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA) has not been reformed ⚠️ verify, and its own regulator is calling for modernised laws ⚠️ verify.

Since May, enforcement has moved; statutes have not. This issue argues that, for the next year at least, a Canadian privacy programme should be built to the regulators' findings, not to the bills.

## What happened

### Ottawa's reform bill is ambitious on paper and stalled in practice

Start with the law everyone is waiting for. C-36, the Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act, would replace PIPEDA's privacy part and create a new regulator, the Digital Safety and Data Protection Commission of Canada. It sets penalties at the greater of $10M or 3% of prior-year global revenue, adds a private right of action, and requires businesses to publicly describe automated decision systems with legal or similarly significant effects. It has no AI part. [Bill text](https://www.parl.ca/DocumentViewer/en/45-1/bill/C-36/first-reading)

LEGISinfo, Parliament's bill tracker, shows no second-reading activity since first reading. [Source](https://www.parl.ca/legisinfo/en/bill/45-1/c-36) C-36 still needs second reading, committee, the Senate, Royal Assent and a commencement order. Until then, PIPEDA and the OPC set the rules.

**Takeaway:** Map the cheap items to C-36 now, such as an automated-decision register, but keep its timing out of the budget.

**Open question:** Will the revenue-linked penalties and private right of action survive committee?

### Regulators are setting the AI standard through findings

The regulators did not wait for Parliament. On 2026-05-06 a joint investigation by the OPC and the Québec, BC and Alberta commissioners found that OpenAI's initial training of ChatGPT, its AI chatbot, did not comply, citing overcollection and no valid consent or transparency. The OPC found the federal complaint justified and conditionally resolved after OpenAI limited personal and sensitive data in training. [Source](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-news/news-and-announcements/2026/nr-c_260506/)

The Grok finding of 2026-06-11 was harsher. The OPC found that X Corp. and X.AI LLC, the companies behind the X platform and its Grok AI tool, had no valid express consent for generating sexualized deepfakes. It found the complaint justified and not resolved, and recommended suspending Grok Imagine (Grok's image generator), annual third-party audits and six months of circumvention monitoring. The companies declined the suspension but committed to four other recommendations and quarterly reports. [Source](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/investigations/investigations-into-businesses/2026/pipeda-2026-004/)

On 2026-09-10 the OPC added guidance on third-party service providers: accountability stays with the business. [Source](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-news/news-and-announcements/2026/nr-c_260910/) The OPC cannot issue binding orders, which is why it filed a Federal Court application against Google on 2026-08-28 to implement its de-listing recommendations. [Source](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-news/news-and-announcements/2026/nr-c_260828/) PIPEDA complaints reached 3,044 in 2025-26, up 109%. [Source](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-news/news-and-announcements/2026/nr-c_260604/) All of this applies today's PIPEDA, without C-36.

**Takeaway:** The OPC's Grok recommendations (audits, monitoring, progress reports) are a ready-made control set for any generative AI feature.

**Open question:** If a company can decline a recommended suspension, as X did, how much weight will boards give a finding?

### Québec's Law 25 is fully live, but its penalties are untested

Québec already has the modern law Ottawa is still debating. Law 25 has applied in full since 2024-09-22, with administrative penalties up to the greater of C$10M or 2% of worldwide turnover ⚠️ verify. [Osler](https://www.osler.com/fr/articles/mises-%C3%A0-jour/loi-25-nouveau-regime-d-application-de-la-loi-quebecoise-sur-la-protection-des-renseignements-pers/) No 2026 penalty was found, and blog claims that penalties have begun are unconfirmed ⚠️ verify.

Québec's privacy regulator, the Commission d'accès à l'information (CAI), joined the OpenAI investigation. Its five-year report, tabled 2026-06-11, proposes 74 changes, including limits on mass collection to train AI ⚠️ verify. [Bulletin Aylmer](https://bulletinaylmer.com/quebec-s-access-and-privacy-watchdog-seeks-a-sweeping-overhaul-though-its-report-may-go-nowhere-fast) The provincial election on 2026-10-05 makes quick action unlikely ⚠️ verify.

**Takeaway:** Use Law 25 as your Canadian high-water mark, and say "no confirmed penalty", not "no penalty".

**Open question:** When the CAI does fine someone, will the first case be about AI training or about the basics?

### BC's private-sector law has not changed, and its regulator is busier than ever

BC tells the same story without a new statute. BC PIPA is unamended, and no reform bill was found ⚠️ verify. BC's regulator, the Office of the Information and Privacy Commissioner (OIPC), tabled its 2025/26 annual report on 2026-06-25. It calls for modernised laws and reports privacy complaints nearly doubling, from 456 to 904 ⚠️ verify. [OIPC BC](https://oipc.bc.ca/documents/news-releases/3187) The one BC bill in the window, Bill 9, amended BC's public-sector privacy law ⚠️ verify. [Gowling](https://gowlingwlg.com/en/insights-resources/articles/2026/british-columbia-modernizes-freedom-of-information-and-public-sector-access-regime)

**Takeaway:** In BC, the joint OpenAI finding is a better guide than waiting for a PIPA bill.

**Open question:** Can BC run a credible private-sector regime on an old statute while complaints double?

> **This week:** On 2026-10-07 the Privacy Commissioner spoke to a House committee on security screening, a public-sector matter outside PIPEDA ([OPC](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-news/news-and-announcements/2026/nr-c_261007/)). Nothing new was found for C-36, the CAI or BC's OIPC; Parliament's Hansard record and the Canada Gazette were not checked.

## Where I land

Stop waiting for C-36. Parking Canadian privacy work "until the federal bill lands" was a mistake with C-27, and is a bigger one now.

The rules for AI and personal data in Canada are being written in findings. The OpenAI and Grok decisions show what regulators test: consent, appropriate purpose, how much data you took, and how fast you respond. Four regulators investigated OpenAI together, each under its own law. That is the closest thing Canada has to a national AI privacy standard.

I would build the programme around one artefact: an inventory of vendors and AI systems, with data categories, consent basis and contract terms. It answers the OPC's service-provider guidance today and maps to C-36's automated-decision transparency if the bill passes. I treat Law 25 as the ceiling for design choices and the OPC findings as the floor for evidence.

The trade-off I accept is that some of this work may not match C-36's final text. I would rather rework a register later than explain why there is no record of where training data came from. The OPC cannot order compliance, but it can go to court, and complaints are rising. Acting before the law settles has a cost, and I think it is the smaller one.

## The questions still open

- Whether non-binding findings change behaviour: X committed to some recommendations but declined the suspension of Grok Imagine.
- How far the provinces diverge: Québec's regulator wants 74 changes, BC and Alberta have not reformed their private-sector laws ⚠️ verify, and each applies its own statute.
- What C-36 looks like after committee: its penalties and private right of action make it matter, and they are the parts I expect industry lobbying to target.

## What to do this quarter

- Add AI training-data provenance and consent questions to your privacy impact assessment (PIA) template.
- Build one inventory of vendors and AI systems, and comment on the OPC service-provider guidance by 2026-12-04 if it affects you.
- Start an automated-decision-system register; it maps to C-36 and costs little.
- Mark provincial dates "to verify" until confirmed against official statute sites.

### If you work in…

| Sector | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Tech | The OpenAI and Grok findings test AI consent under today's PIPEDA. | Test generative AI features against the OPC's Grok recommendations. |
| Product / SaaS | Your customers stay accountable for how you handle their data. | Keep an answer pack: data map, sub-processors, AI use, PIA. |
| Cloud | I expect customers' vendor due diligence to reach hosting providers. | Document where Canadian personal data sits. |
| Banking / FinServ | OSFI, the federal financial regulator, treats AI as models under Guideline E-23 for federally regulated financial institutions from 2027-05-01 ([OSFI](https://www.osfi-bsif.gc.ca/en/guidance/guidance-library/guideline-e-23-model-risk-management-2027)). | Fold third-party AI into the same inventory. |
| Retail | The OPC is investigating the IDScan.net breach, in which driver's licence and ID scans were stolen ([OPC](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-news/news-and-announcements/2026/nr-c_260921/)). | Review which ID data you keep and which vendor holds it. |
