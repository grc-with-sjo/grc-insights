## In brief

- On 27 October the AICPA released draft 2027 SOC 2 criteria with new points of focus on AI: prompt injection, data poisoning, model drift, bias, AI-generated output and AI agents' access. Comments close on 15 January 2027; if approved, the criteria apply to engagements beginning on or after 31 December 2027.
- Every AI addition is a point of focus, which the AICPA says "does not require a specific control". A vendor can still receive a clean report without addressing any of them.
- The draft concentrates on AI inside the product. It says little about AI used to build the product, such as AI-written code and coding agents in the pipeline, which is where much vendor AI now sits.

## The story so far

A SOC 2 report is the document most of us ask a vendor for first. A licensed CPA examines whether the vendor's controls meet the AICPA's Trust Services Criteria. The current criteria date from 2017, with points of focus refreshed in 2022, and they do not mention AI.

When I review a vendor today, the SOC 2 report answers a question I am no longer mainly asking. It tells me access is reviewed and changes are approved. It does not tell me whether the vendor uses AI to run its operations, to build its software, or inside the product I am buying, and those carry very different risks.

The 2027 draft is the AICPA's first real answer. It is late, and it is welcome. This issue argues that it names the right risks but does not yet make anyone show they control them, so buyers need to ask for more alongside it.

## What happened

### The draft names the right AI risks

Credit first: the list is good. The AI material is spread across the existing criteria rather than added as a new section. It asks entities to consider "training data manipulations, adversarial inputs, and model misuse" and "reliability, bias, and potential unintended consequences of AI outputs, prompt manipulation, and data poisoning". Users should be "informed when outputs are generated by AI components of the system". Access reviews should catch "AI or other technology accounts that do not need access", and controls should detect unauthorized "AI tools and models". Mitigation of "prompt injection, jailbreak chaining, and data poisoning" appears, as does monitoring for drift, "bias amplification, unexpected model outputs, or unintended behaviors". Vendor reviews now name "AI models, APIs, or plugins" as external components, and the privacy criteria add that personal information is "not improperly retained or exposed by AI models". [AICPA exposure draft](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/news/article/aicpa-proposes-update-of-soc-2-r-trust-services-criteria-to-enhance-the-relevance-usability-and-value-of-cpas-reports)

That reads like it was written by people who have watched AI incidents happen.

**Takeaway:** The draft's AI points of focus are the clearest plain-language list of AI risks an auditor will eventually ask about; it is worth using as a checklist now.

**Open question:** Will auditors treat these points as the expected baseline, or as optional extras a vendor can leave out of scope?

### None of it is required, and the strongest controls are optional

The draft says it plainly: use of the criteria "does not require an assessment of whether each point of focus is addressed", and "each point of focus does not require a specific control". The AI items guide management and the auditor; they are not tests. The AICPA's guidance on applying the criteria to systems with AI components is listed as "currently under development".

The most concrete AI controls sit in processing integrity: data lineage for training data, "pre-deployment validation, bias testing, and revalidation upon retraining or material change", and "human review or override" of AI output. Processing integrity is a category the vendor chooses whether to include, and the draft notes that security is the category addressed in most engagements. A report scoped to security alone never reaches those controls.

Timing adds to the gap. If approved next year, the criteria apply to engagements beginning on or after 31 December 2027, with early adoption permitted. Most reports you receive through 2027 will be tested against criteria that do not mention AI.

**Takeaway:** A clean 2027-criteria SOC 2 report will not, by itself, show that a vendor controls its AI risks; check which categories are in scope and which AI points of focus were addressed.

**Open question:** How many vendors will adopt early, and will customers ask them to?

### AI used to build the product is mostly missing

This is the gap I run into most. The change-management points of focus (authorise, test, approve and deploy changes) do not mention AI. Nothing addresses AI-written code, coding agents with commit rights, or how AI-assisted changes are reviewed before they reach production. What touches the build is indirect: access for AI agents, detection of unsanctioned AI tools, and "AI-enabled techniques" for vulnerability scanning.

Most AI points of focus are anchored to "AI components of the system", which is the product in scope, not the process that builds it. The report also still does not have to say where AI is used, so a buyer cannot tell whether AI runs the vendor's operations, writes its code or sits in the customer's data path. The AICPA released a separate draft on description criteria the same day; whether it closes this gap is the next thing I will read.

**Takeaway:** Ask vendors directly where they use AI, in operations, in building software, and in the product, because the report will not say.

**Open question:** Should a SOC 2 system description be required to disclose AI used to develop the system, not only AI inside it?

### Other frameworks cover parts of the gap

No single framework closes it, but several are more direct. ISO/IEC 42001 certifies an AI management system: governance, roles and controls for how an organisation develops and uses AI. ISO/IEC 27090:2026, published in October, is guidance on detecting and mitigating AI-specific threats such as data poisoning and model theft across the AI life cycle; it is guidance, not a certification. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/27090) CSA STAR for AI Level 2, launched on 27 October, adds third-party-verified AI assurance through ISO/IEC 27001 or 42001 plus CSA's AI Controls Matrix. [CSA](https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/press-releases/2026/10/27/cloud-security-alliance-launches-star-for-ai-level-2-to-strengthen-ai-governance-and-trust) AIUC-1 certifies AI agents, includes live testing of agent behaviour and is updated quarterly; the AICPA and AIUC are rolling out a joint SOC 2 and AIUC-1 engagement, with full launch expected in early 2027. [AICPA](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/news/article/auditors-meet-urgent-demand-for-ai-assurance-with-new-offering-from-aicpa-and-aiuc)

That joint offering is telling: the AICPA itself appears to expect SOC 2 alone will not carry AI assurance. None of these, as far as I could check, speaks directly to AI used to build ordinary software.

**Takeaway:** For AI-heavy vendors, ask for SOC 2 plus one AI-specific assurance (ISO/IEC 42001, STAR for AI or AIUC-1), matched to where their AI risk sits.

**Open question:** Which of these will buyers converge on, and will auditors combine them into one report?

## How I'd approach it

The uncertainty I would name first is not whether the draft gets approved. It is how long buyers keep treating a clean SOC 2 as an answer on AI when, through at least 2027, the criteria behind it say nothing about AI.

I would keep asking for the SOC 2 report and stop treating it as the AI answer. For each critical vendor I would ask three things alongside it: where AI is used (operations, build or product); which of the draft's AI points of focus their controls already address; and whether they hold any AI-specific assurance. I would write the answers into the contract, not only the questionnaire.

For my own organisation, I would use the draft's AI points of focus as an internal checklist now, and consider whether processing integrity belongs in scope for any system where AI shapes outputs customers rely on.

The trade-off: more questions slow procurement, and vendors will push back while their auditors are not asking yet. I would rather absorb that friction now than rely on a report that was never designed to look at AI.

## Questions to take to your team

- **For each critical vendor, do you know whether AI sits in their operations, their build process or your data path?** The SOC 2 report will not tell you.
- **Does your own SOC 2 scope include processing integrity?** If not, the draft's most concrete AI controls will not appear in your report.
- **Who reviews code that AI wrote before it reaches production, and what evidence does that review leave?** No version of the criteria asks yet, so the answer is often nobody in particular.

## What to do this quarter

- Add three AI questions to vendor due diligence: where AI is used, which AI points of focus are addressed, and what AI-specific assurance exists.
- Map your own controls against the draft's AI points of focus and note the gaps.
- Confirm whether processing integrity should be in your SOC 2 scope for systems with AI-generated outputs.
- If you rely on SOC 2 reports, consider commenting on the exposure draft before 15 January 2027, especially on AI used in software development.

### If you work in…

| Sector | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Product / SaaS | Customers will start asking about AI before your auditor does. | Prepare a short AI-use statement covering operations, build and product. |
| Cloud | STAR for AI and AIUC-1 offer AI-specific assurance alongside SOC 2. | Decide which, if any, matches your customers' questions. |
| Tech | AI-assisted development is not covered by the draft criteria. | Document how AI-written code is reviewed and approved. |
