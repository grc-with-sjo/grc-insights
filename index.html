7 Compliance Trends Every
Company Must Act On

The compliance landscape has shifted dramatically over the past two years. Regulations once described as "upcoming" are now in force and being enforced. Here are the seven trends shaping GRC programmes in 2026: what's changed, what's still coming, and the actions that separate prepared companies from reactive ones.

The Landscape

These are not predictions. They are active regulatory and operational realities. Each one represents a gap that most organizations still have, and an opportunity for those who are ahead of it.

🤖
Trend 01
AI Governance Has Moved from Guidance to Enforcement

The EU AI Act is the world's first comprehensive AI law — and it is live. Prohibited AI practices have been banned since February 2025. Obligations for General Purpose AI models applied from August 2025. High-risk AI system requirements are phasing in through 2026–2027. ISO 42001, the international standard for AI Management Systems, is now being requested in procurement and vendor due diligence. In Canada, Bill C-27 (which included the Artificial Intelligence and Data Act) died when Parliament prorogued in early 2025. The Carney government has signalled intent to reintroduce privacy and AI legislation, but organizations should not wait.

What to do now:

  • Complete an AI inventory — know what tools your teams are actually using, including shadow AI
  • Assign a risk owner for every AI system that touches customer data or influences decisions
  • Map your AI use against the NIST AI RMF Govern, Map, Measure, Manage functions
  • Draft an Acceptable Use Policy for generative AI — auditors are now asking for this
  • Assess whether ISO 42001 certification is relevant to your supplier relationships
🔗
Trend 02
Third-Party Risk Is Now a Regulatory Obligation, Not Just Best Practice

DORA (the EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act) became applicable to financial entities in January 2025, introducing hard requirements around ICT third-party risk management and concentration risk. The CrowdStrike global outage in July 2024 demonstrated in the starkest possible terms what single-vendor dependency looks like at scale: an estimated USD 10 billion in losses from one defective software update. Meanwhile, SEC cybersecurity disclosure rules are now fully operational. Companies are filing 8-K disclosures within four business days of material incidents, and their vendor relationships are scrutinised in those filings.

What to do now:

  • Tier vendors by data access, operational criticality, and concentration risk
  • Integrate TPRM assessments into procurement — not just annual review cycles
  • Track SOC 2 / ISO 27001 certifications with expiry dates; a lapsed certificate is a finding
  • Map single-vendor dependencies — if one vendor fails, what fails with it?
  • Ensure contracts include right-to-audit, incident notification timelines, and exit rights
🔒
Trend 03
Data Privacy Enforcement Has Arrived — Globally

Canada's PIPEDA is still in force; its replacement (the Consumer Privacy Protection Act, part of Bill C-27) did not pass before Parliament prorogued in early 2025. Quebec's Law 25 is fully in force and enforcement actions are underway. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act is now in effect. In the US, more than 20 states have passed comprehensive privacy laws — the proposed American Privacy Rights Act (APRA) did not pass federally in 2024, leaving companies to navigate a state-by-state patchwork. Brazil's LGPD continues active enforcement. The common thread: regulators are no longer issuing warnings. They are issuing fines.

What to do now:

  • Update your data inventory — know what personal data you hold, where it lives, and how long
  • Review your privacy notice for Quebec Law 25 compliance and align for expected C-27 requirements
  • Build a 72-hour breach notification playbook (C-27 will require this when it passes)
  • Audit your US state data subject request handling — you likely have obligations in 10+ states
  • Map cross-border data transfers and confirm adequate transfer mechanisms are in place
🛡️
Trend 04
Cybersecurity Governance Is Now a Leadership Responsibility

NIST released Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 in February 2024, its first major revision in a decade. The defining change: a new "Govern" function that makes cybersecurity risk management an explicit organizational leadership responsibility, not an IT function. SEC cybersecurity disclosure rules require publicly listed companies to report material incidents within four business days and to describe their risk management processes and board oversight in annual filings. Boards are now asking questions they could not have answered two years ago. Your governance documentation needs to reflect that.

What to do now:

  • Gap-assess your programme against CSF 2.0 — especially the Govern function
  • Ensure your incident response plan includes board notification triggers and disclosure criteria
  • Document how cybersecurity risk is reported to leadership — this is now auditable
  • Review vendor and customer contracts for CSF 2.0 alignment requirements
📊
Trend 05
Annual Testing Is an Audit Finding Waiting to Happen

Point-in-time testing (assessing a control once a year during audit season) finds last year's problems. The expectation from auditors, regulators, and risk committees is shifting toward continuous controls monitoring (CCM): knowing the status of your controls in near real-time, not retroactively. AI-assisted CCM tools have matured significantly in 2025–2026, with automated evidence collection now accepted by major audit firms. Companies still relying on spreadsheet-tracked annual tests are increasingly being asked by auditors to justify the approach.

What to do now:

  • Identify your 10 highest-risk controls — can any be tested automatically or at higher frequency?
  • Start with IT General Controls (GITCs): access reviews, change logs, and backup confirmations are automatable today
  • Build a structured testing calendar so every control is tested at its required frequency — and tracked
  • Move evidence collection out of email and into a system auditors can actually review
🌱
Trend 06
ESG Reporting: Real Momentum, Then Real Headwinds

The ESG reporting story in 2025–2026 is not a straight line. The EU's CSRD is real and in force — large EU public-interest entities are already reporting on FY2024 data. But the EU's Omnibus simplification package (proposed February 2025) significantly changed what comes next: Wave 2 and Wave 3 implementation timelines have been pushed back, and the scope of who must comply has narrowed. In the US, the SEC's climate disclosure rules (released March 2024) were stayed by courts in April 2024, and the new SEC leadership from January 2025 signalled a significant retreat — companies should verify the current status before relying on those rules as a compliance driver. IFRS S1 and S2 are being adopted in multiple jurisdictions, and Canada's CSA is consulting on alignment. What has not changed: the assurance standard is here. IAASB's ISSA 5000, published in 2024, sets the framework for independent sustainability assurance — the infrastructure question of how you'll be ready when your timeline arrives is the right thing to be solving now.

What to do now:

  • Confirm your CSRD wave — don't assume; the Omnibus changes affect who is in scope and when
  • Build the ESG data infrastructure regardless of regulatory timeline — readiness takes 18+ months
  • Apply the same internal controls rigour to sustainability data as you do to financial data
  • Monitor the SEC climate rule status if you have US public company obligations — the landscape has shifted
  • Ask your auditors now what assurance will look like under ISSA 5000 for your organization
⚙️
Trend 07
GRC Technology Has Crossed the Line from Tracking to Doing

The GRC platform of 2022 was an evidence locker and audit tracker. The GRC platform of 2026 uses agentic AI to flag control drift before it becomes a failure, auto-populate evidence from integrated systems, draft policy exception write-ups, and surface emerging regulatory risks from live feeds. Purpose-built tools for smaller organizations (Vanta, Drata, and newer entrants) have made automated compliance accessible without enterprise budgets. Companies still running compliance on spreadsheets face two problems: a capacity problem as obligations multiply, and an audit defensibility problem as auditors scrutinise the reliability of manually maintained records.

What to do now:

  • Audit your current GRC tooling — is it helping you manage risk, or just document it after the fact?
  • Identify processes that are manual today but automatable: access reviews, policy attestation, evidence collection
  • Evaluate purpose-built tools on three dimensions: cost, risk reduction, and audit defensibility
  • Build the business case — every material control failure costs more than the tooling that could have prevented it
The Bottom Line

Compliance is no longer a once-a-year event.

The organizations navigating this regulatory environment are the ones that have built compliance into daily operations — not those scrambling to reconstruct evidence before an audit. The seven trends above share a common thread: regulators now expect you to know your risks, own your controls, and prove it continuously. The frameworks, standards, and tools to do this are mature and available. The question is whether your programme is designed to use them.
