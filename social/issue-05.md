Title: AIUC-1 certifies the AI agent. The risk still sits in what you let it do.

Body (condensed, not the full issue; no tables, which LinkedIn articles cannot render):

AI agents act, and the assurance we lean on was not built for that. This issue looks at AIUC-1, the standard that ties agent testing to insurance, and what it does and does not tell you.

**In brief**
- AIUC-1 is a standard for AI agents. The company behind it writes the standard, runs the tests, issues the certificate and sells insurance.
- The body behind SOC 2 reports is rolling out a joint SOC 2 and AIUC-1 engagement with several accounting firms, and expects a full launch in early 2027.
- A certificate covers one product, at one version of the standard, for one year. It says nothing about the access your own team gives that agent.

**What happened**

**One company, every role.** AIUC finalises the standard, runs the technical evaluations, accredits the auditors and issues the certificate. Lenny Zeltser, relaying Zack Korman, has argued this creates conflicts of interest; AIUC's answer, as Zeltser reports it, is that it pays when certified agents fail. An advisory group on governance reports with the 15 January 2027 release.

**The AICPA is rolling it out.** Several CPA firms are delivering a joint SOC 2 plus AIUC-1 engagement, with full launch expected in early 2027. The AICPA release names no firms; AIUC's own page lists nine and says AIUC or an accredited evaluation body runs the quarterly testing.

**Insurance gives it teeth.** ElevenLabs says its voice-agent cover is enabled through AIUC-1 certification. AIUC's pages do not name the insurer or the limits.

**How I'd approach it**

AIUC-1 is the most useful thing on the market for testing how an agent behaves. It is still a test of the vendor's agent, in the vendor's set-up.

My exposure starts after that: the systems the agent can reach and the actions that need a person to approve them are my settings. I would also ask first what happens when the vendor swaps the underlying model between quarterly evaluations.

The trade-off is time. I would rather spend it than inherit an agent's permissions by default.

**Questions to take to your team**
- For each AI agent you have approved, who decided what it is allowed to do, and where is that written down?
- If a vendor shows you an AIUC-1 certificate, could you name the product, the standard version and the date of its last evaluation?
- Which of your insurance policies would respond to a loss caused by an AI agent?

**What to do this quarter**
- Map every AI agent to its owner, permissions and approval steps.
- Ask vendors citing AIUC-1 for scope, standard version and latest evaluation results.
- Add contract notice and re-testing when the underlying model changes.
- Confirm with your broker how current policies treat AI-caused losses.

## Community summary (optional: only if it adds value; no link-dumping)

AIUC-1 is a quarterly-updated standard for AI agents from a company that also insures them, and the AICPA is rolling out a joint SOC 2 + AIUC-1 engagement with full launch expected in early 2027. The certificate is product-scoped, version-locked and lasts a year with quarterly retests, and AIUC currently runs the agent tests itself. The practical gap for buyers is the access their own teams grant: the certificate does not cover your configuration.
